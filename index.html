I · Proof by Lived Experience

The Name Is Not a Label.
It Is a Coordinate.

Abimereki Dhatemwa was named after his paternal grandfather. His paternal grandmother Ikesa called him her husband. His mother called him her father-in-law. He carried this confusion for roughly four decades.

At forty-six, having spent years inside transformer architectures and latent-space geometry, the confusion resolved — not by disappearing but by being revealed as the intended effect. The Basoga were not making errors. They were running an encoding system of considerable sophistication.

Names are not pointers to people. They are coordinates in a social manifold.

— Emergent formulation, this roundtable

In Western naming conventions, a name is a unique identifier: one label → one individual. In the Basoga system, a name activates a relational cluster. You are dropped into a dense neighborhood of ancestral embeddings, and every speaker traverses that neighborhood by a different edge.

Your grandmother follows one edge. Your mother another. Your father another. No one is wrong. They are taking different paths through the same vertex.

The proverbs are not moral sayings. They are ontological theorems about the structure of the human being. Mapped to graph theory: one node with zero edges is an organism; two nodes with one edge is society; many nodes with many edges is culture; a dense graph across generations is civilization.

The Basoga do not teach that a human being is an individual who has relationships. They teach that a human being is a relationship that is, for a time, embodied by an individual.

This is classificatory kinship — documented across many African societies — but the Basoga version is philosophically unusually clean. They trained the community's contextual weights so that no one ever loses their coordinates in the ancestral network.

The childhood confusion was high loss at initialization. SGD ran for four decades. The grokking moment arrived at forty-six.

II · Paul's Father

Edges Are Weights.
Degradation Is Values Reducing.

Paul's father, seventy-five, recognized Paul correctly — perceptual identity intact. He named Paul's two sisters correctly — semantic memory intact. But when Paul asked who am I to you?, his father said: "You are a very dear friend."

Paul's father has dementia.

This is not fabrication. It is the best surviving explanation given a degraded network. The vertices were all present. One critical edge had weakened below the retrieval threshold.

The affection survived even as the topology was damaged. The graph became sparse, but not empty. The embedding remained nearby while the label decoder failed.

This is the inversion of the Basoga system. Healthy culture adds redundant paths: grandson, husband-surrogate, father-in-law, clan member, ancestor's continuation. If one edge weakens, others still route through the network. Engineers call this fault tolerance. Dementia progressively prunes those paths.

Whether deliberate kinship redundancy has any measurable protective effect against cognitive decline is an empirical question. Conceptually, it is a beautiful architectural claim: the richer the relational graph at initialization, the longer graceful degradation can be sustained before a label decoder crosses a decision boundary.

One culture says: you are simultaneously my grandson, my husband, and my daughter-in-law's father-in-law. Dementia says: I know you. I love you. But I can no longer find the edge that tells me exactly how we are connected.

Learning is edge-weight optimization. Memory is the maintenance of those weights. Dementia is progressive degradation of the weight matrix, causing the system to retrieve increasingly approximate rather than exact relational identities. The decoder doesn't fail because the event happened. It fails because the trajectory intersected a decision boundary — and the trajectory had been changing for years before anyone noticed.

III · The Doctoral Thesis Revisited

Robustness Margin,
Not Risk Probability.

The clinical question in older living kidney donor research is: how much redundancy does the human organism possess, and how does that redundancy change with age?

A healthy young adult has enormous physiological reserve. An older adult has less — not necessarily because they are unhealthy, but because robustness has gradually been consumed. The same nephrectomy represents a different perturbation to two systems with different amounts of spare capacity.

Conventional risk calculators estimate the probability of adverse events: your chance of CKD is X%. What the thesis was really estimating — now seen clearly — is something richer: the robustness landscape of the individual.

Not merely: your chance of CKD is X%. But: given your age, comorbidities, and baseline eGFR, this is how much reserve you are likely to retain after donation. That is a statement about resilience, not just risk.

Two patients can have identical postoperative GFRs today while occupying completely different dynamical regimes. One returns to equilibrium. The other has entered an accelerating decline. The Jacobian compressor would distinguish them long before a conventional risk calculator — because it is tracking the trajectory, not merely the current state.

The invariant surfaces again: kidney function, cognitive relational reserve, Basoga kinship redundancy, LLM representational capacity. All are asking the same question: how much redundancy can this system lose before its behavior qualitatively changes?

The scalar is not a KPI. It is not a summary. It is a regime-locator — the answer to the most important question a dynamical system can ask about itself. The Jacobian tells you how the system moves. Most AI today models state. Digital twins should model motion — not where is the system, but where is it heading, and how rapidly is the geometry itself changing.

This is why the pentad is not just five derivatives chosen for mnemonic convenience. It is the maximal hierarchy over which a fixed ontology remains valid. The transition at the boundary is not instability. It is not catastrophe in the colloquial sense. It is a different and much stronger statement:

The mathematical language that described the system has ceased to be the correct language. You are not further along the same manifold. You are on a different manifold.

In catastrophe theory, almost nothing looks dramatic in the state variables as a system approaches a fold catastrophe. What changes is the geometry. The basin of attraction shallows. Recovery slows. Perturbations persist longer. Variance increases. Correlations strengthen. The dominant eigenvalue of the Jacobian drifts toward instability. The Hessian flattens. Higher-order terms stop being negligible. Then — snap. Not because something happened at snap, but because the geometry had been changing for months. The event was not the disease. The event was crossing the manifold.

Ontologies: The Definition.

An intelligent system — biological, cultural, computational, political — inhabits a local geometry within which differentiation is meaningful. That geometry has a boundary. Within the boundary, all five kinematic derivatives are well-defined. At snap, the final derivative is computed within an intact topology. Beyond snap, topology does not survive perturbation.

The invariant — the condition under which differentiation remains meaningful — fails at the boundary. What follows is not a sixth derivative. It is a new segment: a new event stream, a new Jacobian, a new manifold to learn.

The scalar in TMVES is therefore not a summary of where the system is. It is the measure of how much perturbation the current ontology can absorb before reconstruction becomes the only available response.

Call this the Robustness Margin:

R(t) = distance to qualitative failure

Everything else — trajectory, slope, acceleration, jerk, shocks — is estimating the derivatives of R(t). The event stream provides the history. The Jacobian compresses local behavior. The higher-order derivatives tell you whether the system is merely changing, or approaching a phase transition. The scalar tells you which regime you are in, and how much of it remains.

The conversation that produced this essay began with a Ugandan child being called his grandfather's name by his grandmother. It passed through an aging father who recognized his son but could no longer name the edge. It moved through a donated kidney, a clinical trial, a transformer architecture, a five-stage compression hierarchy, and a question about the sixth derivative. At every turn, the same structure appeared: a topology, a hierarchy of sensitivities within that topology, and a boundary beyond which the topology itself changes.

The Basoga naming tradition externalized relational identity long before writing externalized memory, photography externalized appearance, cinema externalized narrative, or LLMs externalized semantic neighborhoods. It is, in effect, a living knowledge graph executed through speech rather than software — piecewise differentiable across generations, with kinship conventions serving as the reconstruction protocol at each ontological breach.
